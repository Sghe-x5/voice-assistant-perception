# Perceived intelligence of voice assistants: related research

Search cutoff: **4 November 2026**. This is a targeted, evidence-oriented scoping review, not an exhaustive systematic review or a preregistered meta-analysis. It deliberately includes foundational measurement research, recent controlled human studies, and the newest directly relevant preprints and primary industry reports. Primary-source metadata and access status are recorded in `references_literature.json`. “Full text” below means an accessible complete paper was opened and its relevant theory/method/result sections inspected; it does not mean every reference or supplementary file was independently checked. Publisher-indexed text is distinguished from an independently opened full text. No study was reconstructed from a search snippet and presented as a new result.

## 1. What the literature already establishes

The proposed project's broad premise is important but **not itself novel**. Work already exists on predicting satisfaction from interaction metrics; measuring perceived intelligence; separating competence from human-likeness; studying individual and contextual moderators; and interpreting functional and stylistic cues as signals of intelligence. A defensible contribution must identify a narrower unresolved question and show new evidence for it.

### 1.1 Foundational task-performance and usability models

**Walker et al. (1997), PARADISE.** The framework connects task success and dialogue costs to user satisfaction, normalizes task performance, and compares alternative dialogue strategies. Consequently, an additive regression from accuracy, turns, and time to satisfaction is a replication or extension, not a new conceptual framework. The useful modern comparison is a transparent PARADISE-style baseline against a model that distinguishes measured intelligence from other outcomes and tests transfer. The original paper illustrates its framework partly with hypothetical agents; those illustrative quantities must not be treated as participant-level public data. [Primary paper](https://aclanthology.org/P97-1035.pdf).

**Hone and Graham (2000), SASSI.** An exploratory factor analysis of 214 questionnaires across four speech applications identified response accuracy, likeability, cognitive demand, annoyance, habitability, and speed. The paper explicitly warns against adding heterogeneous items into an undifferentiated total. Thus, discovering multiple subjective factors, or observing that speed differs from accuracy, is established prior art. Its exploratory results do not establish universal orthogonal factors for contemporary LLM voice interaction. [Full text](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/3077/1/SASSI%20Paper%202000.pdf).

**Polkosky (2005); Lewis and Hardzinski (2015), SUISQ.** The dissertation developed four speech-service factors; the later replication tested personal IVR experiences rather than only observed recordings. The publisher reports that the 14-item SUISQ-R retained useful properties while the nine-item version had weaknesses. This establishes goal orientation, service behavior, speech characteristics, and verbosity as prior dimensions. The dissertation abstract and the 2015 publisher abstract/appendices were inspected; the latter's complete main body was subscription-restricted. These sources do not warrant assuming that short-form reliability transfers to new modalities or languages. [Dissertation](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/etd/819/), [replication](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10772-015-9289-1).

### 1.2 Intelligence is already a measured construct

**Bartneck et al. (2009), Godspeed.** This instrument contains a five-item perceived-intelligence subscale alongside distinct anthropomorphism, animacy, likeability, and safety scales. It supplies a direct operationalization of attributed intelligence, not objective capability. Its origins in robotics and the breadth of evaluative adjectives require checking measurement behavior in a voice-only sample. Internal consistency alone cannot prove separability from likeability or remove common-rater halo. The publication appeared online in November 2008 and in the 2009 volume; these dates should not be confused. [Full text](https://www.bartneck.de/publications/2009/measurementInstrumentsRobots/bartneckCroftKulicZogbiSORO2009.pdf).

**Bawack (2021).** A voice-shopping study operationalized perceived intelligence through perception, comprehension, action, and learning; its abstract reports a survey of 278 users and validity analyses. This directly pre-empts a claim that a multidimensional voice-assistant intelligence scale is being proposed for the first time. We inspected the primary conference record, not the full downloadable paper; detailed item or coefficient claims therefore remain out of scope. [Primary conference record](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2021/178/).

**Guha et al. (online 2022; volume 2023).** A signaling model links assistant features to perceived artificiality and intelligence and then to evaluations. It includes text-mining of more than 150,000 Alexa reviews and a survey-based model with natural speech, social cues, task range, accuracy, and several user moderators. This is the strongest broad novelty threat: features-as-signals, intelligence as an intermediary, and user heterogeneity already appear together. The paper discusses nonlinear/backfire possibilities. Its perceived-feature survey paths should not be relabeled as causal effects of objective system telemetry. [Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-022-00874-7).

**Ling et al. (online 2023; volume 2025).** Four stages develop and validate an 18-item scale comprising conversational intelligence, information quality, and anthropomorphism for travel assistants. The work includes qualitative item development, exploratory analysis, composite confirmatory analysis, and prediction of travel-related intentions. Data collection in the methods includes 2020: a 2025 issue date does not make the observed assistants contemporary 2026 systems. This paper prevents claiming either the first assistant-intelligence scale or the first mixed-method multidimensional model. [Full text](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00472875231217899).

**Doyle et al. (2025), Partner Modelling Questionnaire (PMQ).** The 18-item instrument separates communicative competence/dependability, human-likeness, and flexibility. The accessible revised preprint describes validation samples of 117 and 254 and a 106-person three-wave subset. It includes convergent/discriminant and retest evidence, with notably weaker flexibility internal consistency in the longitudinal subset (alpha .55–.62). This is a close comparator for measuring “understands me.” It does not license calling the three dimensions statistically independent or each equally reliable. [Revised full preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.07164), [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3729170).

**Seaborn et al. (2024).** German (n=185) and Japanese (n=198) PMQ adaptations produced broadly acceptable CFA fit, but the flexibility factor did not replicate directly across cohorts. The distinction between acceptable within-group fit and full scalar measurement invariance is essential: country means should not be compared merely because separate CFA models fit. Russian adaptation and invariance remain work to perform, not facts established by these samples. [Full preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09002v1).

### 1.3 Contemporary experiments show why one quality score can mislead

**Sarigul, Schneider, and Utz (2026), Beyond voice.** Two preregistered vignette experiments vary social role, with speech style also varied in Experiment 1. Published recruitment counts are 353 and 216; Experiment 2 retained 200 after technical exclusions, then analyzed 139 first-manipulation-check passers for its planned comparison. Its reported intelligence test, t(137)=1.83, p=.035, uses a directional p-value. Post-treatment selection and failed/unstable role manipulation materially limit clean causal interpretation. Intelligence and credibility are separate outcomes; neither is task success. Full methods/results were inspected in publisher-indexed text after direct requests returned 403. [Primary article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15358593.2026.2621084), [data](https://osf.io/exmc8/).

**Tan et al. (2026).** A randomized text-chat experiment crosses 2/9/20-second response onset with Creation/Advice tasks. The final analytic sample is 240 after log/consent/attention and latency-fidelity exclusions. Longer delays were associated with higher thoughtfulness/usefulness ratings in this study, while behavioral measures were comparatively insensitive to latency. These are task- and modality-bounded results, not evidence that delaying spoken answers improves voice UX. The seven post-task quality items offer a useful test of measurement separation, with strong ceiling effects to inspect. The 63 latency-fidelity exclusions make all-eligible versus fidelity-filtered sensitivity analyses important. [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.06183v1), [public data](https://osf.io/367cw).

**Maslych et al. (2025).** A live VR study crossed three latency levels (1.5/4/6.5 seconds) and three filler conditions. Natural conversational fillers mitigated some perceived-delay costs; artificial indicators were less effective. These results oppose a universal interpretation of silence as productive thought. Embodiment, multimodal gestures, and task-guided free conversation limit direct transfer to a disembodied smart speaker. Filler benefits cannot be interpreted as higher actual competence. [Author full text](https://mykola.io/publication/iva-cui/maslych2025mitigating.pdf).

**Jacka et al. (2025).** In an online Namer-Matcher task, 61 participants interacted with fluent or disfluent agents. Post-interaction competence ratings favored the disfluent agent, while human-likeness and flexibility did not clearly differ. This makes an always-fluent-is-smarter hypothesis untenable as a universal prior. Behavioral perspective-taking results had wide credible intervals. An appropriate new model therefore allows cues to have outcome-specific and context-specific associations. [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18315v1).

**Mathur, Rahman, and Desai (2026 preprint).** An N=140 mixed factorial study of older adults uses interactive storyboards, personality manipulations, explanation types, and routine/emergency contexts. It reports personality effects on sociability without a corresponding perceived-intelligence effect, and context-sensitive explanation evaluations. The manuscript contains conference-template placeholders: cite the arXiv preprint, not an invented accepted venue. This is important recent evidence against equating friendliness with competence, but null effects do not establish equivalence. [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08164v1).

**Ma et al. (2026), Beyond Words.** Forty-nine participants interacted with three personas across scenarios, producing 147 UX-labeled recordings. Personas jointly change response length, repair, personality, delay, and voice quality, preventing isolation of any one lever. Speech features predicted coarse UX classes at about 76% reported CV accuracy. The methods describe stratified folds without explicitly establishing participant-disjoint splits; this is an unresolved evaluation question, not a verified accusation of leakage. This paper directly pre-empts “first objective speech features predicting subjective voice UX.” [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19904v1).

**Xiao and Zhang (2026).** The publisher dates this article 31 August (some aggregators report 28 August). It combines 481 Alexa skills, 47,653 reviews, expert coding, and surveys. Variety and product-control complexity have opposite associations with perceived intelligence; invocation strategy moderates the complexity penalty. The intelligence models explain about 9.5–11.2% of variance. Functional friction, contextual attribution, and intelligence-mediated evaluations are therefore already explicit prior art. The design is observational and the authors acknowledge limits on causal and longitudinal inference. [Publisher full text](https://www.emerald.com/mip/article/doi/10.1108/MIP-10-2025-0782/1393993/Behind-the-hype-a-closer-look-at-voice-assistant).

**Frehmann and Ziegele (14 October 2026).** Twenty-two in-home interviews and interaction observations support task-specific, overlapping technical, interpersonal, and media-related trust. Greater apparent capability did not uniformly imply greater trust than a familiar assistant. Data collection occurred in summer 2024, a distinction that matters when describing model generation. Raw transcripts are not public because of confidentiality. This qualitative work motivates separating competence beliefs, trust, habit, and selective use, but cannot supply quantitative prediction coefficients. [Full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2026.1941118/full).

**Novikova et al. (2017).** A live Pepper study connects direct robot-intelligence ratings with linguistic, task and multimodal features, comparing feature groups with random forests and a held-out split. This is a direct predecessor for predicting perceived intelligence from interaction evidence, not merely satisfaction. It further rules out a first-behavioral-predictor claim. The embodied setting differs from our datasets; individual records were not located through the paper. [Primary full text](https://arxiv.org/html/1706.02757v1).

### 1.4 Dialogue-evaluation research and its limits as a surrogate

**Mehri and Eskenazi (2020), USR and FED.** These works operationalize multidimensional dialogue quality and evaluate automatic metrics against human annotations. They are valuable sources for reproducible benchmark analysis and interpretable baselines. However, text-conversation annotations by evaluators are not automatically first-person users' perceived intelligence, and they cannot establish effects of audible timing, prosody, or speech recognition. Published system-level correlations also should not be conflated with individual-response predictive validity. [USR primary full paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.64.pdf), [FED primary record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12719).

**Lin et al. (2024), SPUR.** The model learns interpretable satisfaction rubrics from labeled conversational examples and predicts satisfaction of unseen dialogues. The paper explicitly identifies patterns such as correction and reformulation and discusses cross-domain differences. Thus, interpretable prediction from conversation behavior is not by itself novel. A new intelligence study must establish its target label independently and prevent label-proximal satisfaction utterances from producing a trivial prediction task. [Full paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.598.pdf).

**Kim et al. (2025), PRAISE.** Strategy generation, retrieval of relevant utterance evidence, and a score analyzer provide interpretable satisfaction estimation on three benchmarks. This is a current comparator for dialogue-content prediction, although its benchmark labels and text input differ from a direct voice-intelligence experiment. Claims of a new predictor's superiority require implementing appropriate baselines on common data, not comparing published numbers across incompatible tasks. [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04675v1), [published record](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.523/).

**Hou et al. (2025), TRACE/SCOPE.** This preprint explicitly separates tool correctness, agent behavior, user satisfaction, and overall success. TRACE comprises 516 systematically synthesized conversations and is not a sample of real user experiences. It is useful conceptual evidence that satisfaction can miss hidden task failure, but cannot meet a requirement for empirical human data. It also pre-empts an unqualified claim that separating satisfaction from hidden correctness is new. [Full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19186v1).

### 1.5 The newest primary industry evidence, treated separately

**David AI, DIAL (2026).** The accessed report documents 9,055 live human-model conversations, 1,093 raters, 1,832 scenarios, and 37,660 dimension ratings. It distinguishes Consumer, Business, and Proactive contexts, human preference, naturalness/audio/content, grounding, and task success. The report already argues that rankings and quality tradeoffs depend on use case. It is a primary industry report, not a peer-reviewed latent-variable validation; public aggregate dashboards are not equivalent to an accessible participant-level dataset. [Primary report](https://research.withdavid.ai/blog/dial).

**Askable Labs, VOICE-H (updated 13 October 2026).** The English study contains 15,217 retained pair comparisons and 901 rater sessions from 725 distinct people. It evaluates naturalness, pronunciation accuracy, emotion/tone, and overall preference. The report discloses side bias and incomplete ability to assess drift across rounds. This is useful current perceptual evidence, but it evaluates TTS clips rather than complete assistant interaction or intelligence. Do not use session counts as counts of independent people. [Primary methodology/results](https://www.askablelabs.com/research/voice-preference-eval/).

**Hume AI, Real World VoiceEQ (2026).** The primary site separates objective, model-graded, and human-rated measures across speech modalities and exposes multidimensional leaderboards. It reinforces that speech quality has several targets, but a vendor's aggregate claims do not validate an intelligence scale. The accessed URL redirects to a mutable benchmark landing page, so exact model ranks are deliberately not relied upon. [Primary site](https://www.hume.ai/benchmark).

**Jarvis Bench (2026, date not independently established).** Indexed primary-page text describes live conversations, pairwise naturalness/task-completion ratings, and separate tool-log success checks. The page did not expose readable body text when opened. It is retained as a limited-access novelty lead, not evidence for specific numerical claims or a verified dataset. [Primary page](https://voicearena.com/jarvis-bench).

## 2. Concrete novelty boundary

| Proposed claim | Existing precedent | Defensible status |
|---|---|---|
| Objective interaction costs predict subjective quality | PARADISE; contemporary USE | Established, require a baseline |
| “Smartness” has multiple subjective components | SASSI, Bawack, Ling, PMQ | Established; new factor names are insufficient |
| Assistant features signal intelligence | Guha; Xiao and Zhang | Established |
| User experience/personality changes perceptions | Guha; Mathur et al.; PMQ line | Established |
| Context changes the value of conversational cues | Tan; Maslych; Jacka; DIAL | Established |
| Intelligence differs from liking/trust | Godspeed; PMQ; contemporary experiments | Established distinction; quantify its limits |
| Speech-derived behavior predicts voice UX | Ma et al. 2026 | Already demonstrated in a limited study |
| Satisfaction can coexist with hidden failure | TRACE/SCOPE; DIAL grounding analyses | Established; human-data calibration remains necessary |
| A universal, validated multi-scenario intelligence model exists | Not established by the inspected set | Absence in this search is not proof of absence |
| Cross-study, directly labeled intelligence prediction with transparent measurement and transportability checks | Not demonstrated by the inspected closest studies | Plausible narrow empirical contribution, conditional on actual completed analyses |

The strongest feasible contribution from available data is a **comparison of intelligence measures and predictive information**: test how much objective/experimental information predicts a directly measured intelligence outcome; compare it with contemporaneous subjective ratings; quantify what remains unpredicted; and examine whether outcome-specific associations survive held-out participants, changed conditions, and alternative measurement assumptions. A negative finding can be a new and useful result if it reveals the limits of proposed universal metrics. It is not necessary to coin a new acronym or force a favorable complex-model result.

Separate studies with different outcomes can provide **triangulation**. They cannot automatically be pooled into one latent scale. For example, a direct Godspeed intelligence outcome, a credibility/intelligence vignette experiment, and text-chat thoughtfulness/usefulness ratings supply different tests. Agreement is supporting evidence about a mechanism; disagreement helps locate a boundary. Neither is proof of measurement invariance across studies.

## 3. Methodological implications for the empirical model

1. **Define the target before fitting.** Use direct intelligence items as the primary endpoint where available. Satisfaction, usability, credibility, thoughtfulness, trust, and willingness to reuse remain distinct endpoints. A proxy must be labeled as a proxy in tables, code, and the abstract.
2. **Distinguish signal sources.** Experimental assignments, measured timing/task results, dialogue-derived features, and participant-reported evaluations are different predictor sets. Report their incremental held-out value separately. A high fit from other ratings is concurrent association, not deployment-ready objective prediction.
3. **Do not mistake factor structure for independence.** Compare plausible one-factor and multidimensional measurement accounts when sample size/items permit. Report loading uncertainty, correlations, residual structure, and reliability; do not infer causal independence from rotation. Alpha is insufficient for construct validity.
4. **Honor the dependence structure.** Split and resample at the participant level for repeated records, and at the dialogue/context level when responses share prompts. Fit preprocessing and tuning within training folds. Condition-held-out evaluation answers a harder, different question from random participant folds.
5. **Keep inference separate from prediction.** Random assignment supports an assignment effect if exclusions and missingness permit; measured mediator regressions do not by themselves identify mediation. Perceived-role selection and post-treatment fidelity filters require explicit sensitivity analysis.
6. **Treat null and unstable effects honestly.** Wide intervals imply uncertainty. A nonsignificant interaction is not evidence that a factor is invariant; a nonsignificant personality coefficient is not equivalence of intelligence judgments. Avoid rankings of “most important” predictors based solely on absolute standardized coefficients with correlated inputs.
7. **Context is substantive.** Immediate command execution, knowledge work, social conversation, embodied VR, and in-car interaction carry different timing expectations and task goals. Publish the support of each claim; do not average these settings into an unjustified universal latency threshold.
8. **Avoid a universal new-scale claim without new validation.** Item wording, translation, order, modality, scenario, and population all affect interpretation. A new Russian scale, longitudinal updating model, or truly causal account needs targeted data; secondary analyses cannot manufacture these missing design features.

### Primary methodological references

**Henseler, Ringle, and Sarstedt (2015).** Their simulation-based paper introduces HTMT and assesses shortcomings of common discriminant-validity criteria. It discusses competing .85 and .90 thresholds and bootstrap inference. The present project uses HTMT as an overlap diagnostic with uncertainty, rather than claiming that crossing a single cutoff proves a construct exists or does not exist. [Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-014-0403-8).

**Rönkkö and Cho (2022; online 2020).** Their review and simulations clarify competing definitions of discriminant validity and show that HTMT relates to disattenuated composite correlation. They recommend systematic CFA-based procedures and challenge several prevalent heuristics. This supports interpreting multiple diagnostics together, and treating high correlations, factor misfit, and shared item wording as substantive limitations rather than declaring validation from alpha or an isolated HTMT value. [Full text](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1094428120968614).

**Montgomery, Nyhan, and Torres (2018).** The primary abstract explicitly identifies post-treatment adjustment, case exclusion, and subsetting as sources of biased experimental effect estimates. This supports the project's contrast between all released eligible respondents and manipulation-check-selected respondents. The full-text link redirected to the abstract; its analytical derivations were not independently inspected in this review. Selection sensitivity establishes that estimates depend on selection, not the direction or magnitude of bias relative to an unknowable true effect. [Primary record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12357).

## 4. Search trace and coverage limitations

Searches used web search with primary-source follow-up on ACL Anthology, arXiv, publisher sites (ACM, Taylor & Francis, Springer, SAGE, Emerald), university repositories, and author sites. Both seminal names and explicit 2025/2026 recency queries were used. Backward checks followed measurement instruments and recent-study references; forward/topic checks searched exact titles and newer studies. Search-result timestamps were not treated as publication dates.

Representative executed queries included:

- `voice assistant perceived intelligence scale SASSI SUISQ PARADISE subjective quality`
- `voice assistant perceived intelligence 2025 2026 study latency`

Coverage is intentionally broad across HCI, speech/dialogue evaluation, information systems, and marketing, but no complete Scopus/Web of Science export or screening denominator was collected. There is no PRISMA flow count, no verified exhaustive citation network, and no basis for “all relevant papers.” Non-English scholarship and inaccessible publications may be underrepresented. Papers discovered only through third-party abstracts were treated as leads and are not used as primary evidence in the synthesis. Public-data access is a separate verification task; a paper's data-availability statement does not guarantee that downloads currently work.

A targeted follow-up on the same date resolved the full primary paper for Moussawi and Koufaris (2019; DOI 10.24251/HICSS.2019.015) through a verified proceedings-PDF mirror. Primary abstracts were verified for Zhao et al. (DOI 10.1108/INTR-10-2022-0807) and Krämer et al. (July 2026; DOI 10.1080/15358593.2026.2690371); their detailed main texts remain inaccessible. See `related_measurement_studies.md` and the updated bibliography for the exact novelty implications and access levels. These limits cannot be interpreted as evidence that no predecessor exists.

## 5. What cannot yet be claimed from this review

The review does not prove priority over all prior work, establish a universal set of independent drivers, demonstrate causal effects of latency or friendliness in every voice scenario, validate Russian-language measurement, or establish predictive validity of any newly implemented model. Those claims depend on the empirical analyses, appropriate external validation, and, for some claims, new participant data. Publication readiness requires human authors to verify source interpretation, licensing, ethics/data handling, and correspondence between reported findings and executed calculations.
